# EP01 — Why It Sucks to Be Born as a Victorian Chimney Sweep

**Format:** told to "you" (second person), from birth to the end of the career, with dark humour and a sad ending.
**Length:** about 1,550 words, roughly 8 minutes at a calm pace.
**Rule:** every fact marked [S#] has to match a source listed at the bottom. Lines with no tag are jokes or reasonable scene-setting, not claims.

---

## COLD OPEN (0:00–0:25)
You are seven years old. You're inside a chimney, in the dark. The tunnel around you is about the width of a dinner plate [S1], and your knees are pressed up against your chin.

You can't go up and you can't go down.

Let's go back to how you got here.

## 1 — BORN (0:25–1:10)
London, 1862. You arrive in a single rented room you share with your parents, four siblings and a damp patch on the wall that has more personality than your dad.

Fun fact about 1862: a law passed twenty-two years before you were born already says nobody under twenty-one is allowed to climb a chimney [S2].

Hold on to that. It's going to be hilarious later.

Your family is poor. Dinner is bread, and on a good day there's also bread. By the time you can walk, your parents are looking at you the way people look at an old coat: maybe there's some money in that.

## 2 — SOLD (1:10–2:00)
Then one morning a man in a black top hat knocks on the door. He's a master sweep, and he likes what he sees.

You're small, you're skinny, and you look like you'd fit in a drainpipe.

Kids like you were often orphans or workhouse boys, or they were simply handed over by parents who needed the money [S3]. The paperwork calls it an "apprenticeship". Your mum calls it "an opportunity".

You call it "why is that man holding my arm."

## 3 — FIRST DAY (2:00–3:10)
Your new home is the master's cellar. Your bed is a pile of soot bags [S4], a mattress made of the stuff you're paid to remove.

The workday starts early. Sometimes your master drags you out of bed at three in the morning [S4], because people want their chimneys clean before they light the morning fires.

Then comes training. There's no school and no safety talk. Here's the chimney, and up you go.

Inside, the flue can narrow to about nine inches by nine inches [S1], smaller than this video's thumbnail on a laptop. There's no ladder and no rope, just you. You push up with your knees and elbows, which isn't a style choice, because there's no room to do anything else [S1].

By the end of week one, your knees and elbows are scraped raw [S1].

By the end of week two, they're still raw. They just stop surprising you.

## 4 — THE ROUTINE (3:10–4:20)
Days turn into a rhythm: wake up in the dark, climb in the dark, breathe soot in the dark.

At the top you sometimes poke your head out of a chimney pot and see the whole of London's rooftops at sunrise. Honestly, it's beautiful, and it's the only window seat you'll ever get.

Then you climb back down, and the master sends you into the next one.

Washing? Not really. Sweeps in your trade were rarely washed [S5]. The soot settles into every fold of your skin and stays there. Remember that too, because it comes back.

## 5 — A MACHINE COULD DO THIS (4:20–5:10)
Here's the part that will make you angry.

A mechanical brush, a long pole with a brush on the end, had existed since around 1803, before your grandparents were even married [S6].

So why are you still climbing? Because plenty of sweeps and their customers just preferred boys [S6]. Boys were cheap, and boys didn't complain to anyone important.

You are, technically, a more expensive option than a stick. You're also a more convenient one, which is worse.

## 6 — THE LAW (5:10–6:00)
In 1864, Parliament passes another Chimney Sweepers Act with fines and everything. Climbing boys are now extra-illegal [S7].

And everyone ignores it [S7].

You're nine. A man from Parliament has decided you shouldn't be here, and your master has decided Parliament can't see inside a chimney.

Your master is right.

## 7 — STUCK (6:00–6:50)
Which brings us back to where we started.

You're inside a flue that bends, your knees are jammed against your chin, and you can't move up or down. This happened to climbing boys again and again. When it ended badly, the coroner often wrote "accidental death" [S1], and nobody went to jail.

Today you're lucky. Your master breaks a few bricks out of the wall and pulls you through by the ankles. He's furious, not because you almost died, but because now he has to pay for the wall.

## 8 — TOO BIG (6:50–7:40)
February 1875. You're thirteen now and getting too big for the flues. Then the newspapers start talking about a boy called George Brewster.

George was a climbing boy too, sent up a chimney at the asylum in Fulbourn, near Cambridge, on 11 February 1875. He got stuck. By the time they pulled him out, it was too late [S8]. His master was charged with manslaughter [S8].

Lord Shaftesbury reads about George and pushes one more law through Parliament. This time sweeps need a licence from the police, and the police actually have to enforce it [S8].

By September 1875 the climbing-boy era in Britain is basically over [S8][S9]. George is often remembered as the last climbing boy to die in England [S8].

You survived it. You're one of the lucky ones, which is a really low bar.

## 9 — THE BILL ARRIVES (7:40–8:20)
Years later you notice a small wart, in a place you'd rather not point at.

Sweeps had a name for it: the soot wart [S10]. In 1775, almost a hundred years before your first climb, a surgeon called Percivall Pott worked out that it was cancer caused by soot sitting in the folds of a sweep's skin [S10]. It's known as the first cancer ever linked to someone's job [S10].

Everybody knew, and the soot bags got used as beds anyway.

## OUTRO (8:20–8:40)
So that's your life: born into a room with a damp patch, sold to a man in a top hat, and sent up chimneys a law had already banned.

The whole time, a brush on a stick could have done your job.

Thanks for living it with us. Next time: why it sucks to be born as a medieval executioner.

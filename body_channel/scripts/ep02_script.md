# EP02 — What Happens to Your Body If You Fall Into the Mariana Trench

_Your Vitals · narration script. Tags like [S3] link each claim to a source below._


## Hook

- `h01` **[10,935 METRES DOWN]** You are lying at the bottom of the deepest place on Earth. Almost eleven kilometres of water are sitting on top of you. [S1]
- `h02` **[8 TONNES / SQUARE INCH]** The pressure down here is about eleven hundred times what you feel on the surface. Roughly eight tonnes on every square inch of your body. [S4]
- `h03` And here's the twist. You're not flat. You're not a little cube. From the outside, you look surprisingly... you. [S5]
- `h04` So what actually happened on the way down? Which parts of you gave up first? And who's that, coming over to say hello? Let's find out, one depth at a time.

## 0 Metres: The Setup

- `s01` **[Above Challenger Deep, near Guam]** You're on a ship in the western Pacific, a few hundred kilometres southwest of Guam. Under you is the Challenger Deep, the deepest known point of the Mariana Trench. [S1]
- `s02` **[Avg ocean: 3,682 m. Here: 10,935 m.]** The average ocean is about three point seven kilometres deep. This spot is about ten point nine. You could drop Mount Everest in here and its peak would still be more than two kilometres underwater. [S1, S16]
- `s03` **[Hypothetical. Do not try.]** Now, people float. So for this experiment, you're holding a very heavy anchor. Don't ask why. You took one big breath, and you fell in.
- `s04` **[SPLASH]** The water up here is warm and tropical. And the second your face goes under, something ancient switches on.

## 10 Metres

- `s05` **[Diving reflex: heart -10 to 25%]** It's called the diving reflex. When cold water touches your face, your heart rate drops by ten to twenty-five percent, and blood vessels in your arms and legs squeeze shut to save oxygen for your brain and heart. [S8]
- `s06` **[10 m = 2x pressure]** Ten metres down, the pressure is already double what it was at the surface. And everything in you that's full of air starts to shrink. [S3, S7]
- `s07` Your ears feel it first. A sharp, stabbing pain, as water pressure pushes on your eardrums. Divers clear their ears every few metres for this reason. You are not clearing anything. [S7, S17]
- `s08` Air-filled spaces get hit hardest. Your middle ears, your sinuses, your lungs, and your gut. Doctors call this barotrauma. You can call it the squeeze. [S7]

## 60 Seconds

- `s09` **[60 SECONDS]** About a minute in. Let's say you're sinking at about one metre per second. That's a bit faster than the first submarine that ever reached the bottom, back in nineteen sixty. [S13]
- `s10` **[CO2 rising]** You're not running out of oxygen yet. What's really hurting is carbon dioxide building up in your blood. Every second, the urge to breathe gets louder. [S6]
- `s11` **[BREAKPOINT]** Then you hit what's called the breath hold breakpoint. You can't hold it any longer. Your body takes a breath anyway. [S6]
- `s12` Sometimes the throat slams shut first, a spasm that tries to keep water out. It doesn't usually last. [S6]
- `s13` With no oxygen reaching your brain, you black out. There's no dramatic warning. It just goes quiet. [S6]
- `s14` Your heart stops. Around six minutes without oxygen, brain damage becomes something nobody recovers from. And from here on, it's just your body, going on a very long trip. [S6]

## 200 Metres: The Last Light

- `s15` **[Sunlight ends here]** At two hundred metres, you pass through the last useful sunlight. Below this point, there isn't enough light for anything to photosynthesize. [S2]
- `s16` **[Deepest breath-hold dive: 214 m]** You've also just passed the deepest anyone has ever gone on a single breath and come back up under their own power, for the record. Herbert Nitsch, two hundred and fourteen metres, in two thousand seven. [S9]
- `s17` **[253 m... and a price]** In two thousand twelve he reached two hundred and fifty-three metres on a sled. Minutes after surfacing, he developed serious decompression sickness. Even the best human ever at this is paying for it here. [S9]

## 700 Metres: Human Limit

- `s18` **[Deepest human under pressure: 701 m]** At seven hundred and one metres, you pass another human record. In nineteen ninety-two, a diver named Theo Mavrostomos reached that pressure in a test chamber in France, breathing a special gas mixture. [S10]
- `s19` He stayed for two hours. Below about a hundred and fifty metres, divers breathing helium mixes can get high pressure nervous syndrome. Tremors. Jerking. Nausea. Their nerves start misfiring under pressure alone. [S10, S11]
- `s20` Not that you're doing much nerve firing right now. A local resident drifts past, takes one look at you, and decides you're not its problem. Yet.

## 1,000 Metres: The Midnight Zone

- `s21` **[MIDNIGHT ZONE]** One kilometre. Total darkness. No sunlight reaches this deep, ever. Scientists call it the midnight zone. [S2]
- `s22` **[5–6 °C]** Up top, the tropical water was above twenty degrees. Down here it's about five or six. The warmth left in your body is draining away into the sea. [S12]
- `s23` **[Lungs: 1/100 of size]** The pressure is now about a hundred atmospheres. Whatever air was left in your lungs has been squeezed to around a hundredth of its surface size. Your chest has caved in around it. [S3, S7]
- `s24` But here's the answer to our big question. Most of you is water, around sixty percent. And water barely compresses at all. Even four kilometres down, it only shrinks by about two percent. [S5, S19]
- `s25` **[60% water. Barely squashable.]** So the deep sea doesn't flatten you like a can. It goes after the air pockets. Lungs, sinuses, ears, gut. The rest of you just gets a firm, permanent hug. [S5, S7]

## 4,000 To 6,000 Metres: The Abyss

- `s26` **[THE ABYSS]** Around an hour in. Four kilometres. This is the abyss, the zone between four and six thousand metres. You're now deeper than the average ocean floor. [S1, S2]
- `s27` **[HADAL ZONE: 6 km+]** And at six kilometres, you enter the hadal zone, named after Hades, the Greek underworld. Only the deep trenches go this far down. [S2]
- `s28` **[Snailfish: 8,178 m]** At about eight thousand metres, you meet the deepest fish ever filmed in the Mariana Trench. A snailfish. Pale, soft, almost see-through. It looks like a tadpole made of jelly. [S14]
- `s29` **[TMAO: pressure armour]** Its trick is a molecule called TMAO, which helps hold its proteins in shape under pressure. The deeper a fish lives, the more of it they carry. [S15]
- `s30` **[Predicted limit for fish: ~8,200 m]** Researchers predicted fish hit a limit around eight thousand two hundred metres. Deeper than that, the chemistry stops working. No fish has ever been found in the deepest quarter of the ocean. [S15]
- `s31` **[Record: 8,336 m (2023)]** The deepest fish ever filmed anywhere was seen in two thousand twenty-three, at eight thousand three hundred and thirty-six metres, in a trench near Japan. Right at the predicted limit. [S14]
- `s32` You, on the other hand, have no TMAO. You've got a breakfast you never got to digest. You keep sinking.

## 9,000 Metres

- `s33` **[Trieste, 1960]** Nine kilometres. In nineteen sixty, two men passed this exact depth inside a submarine called Trieste. And right about here, one of its outer window panes cracked and shook the whole vessel. [S13]
- `s34` **[4 h 47 min to the bottom]** They kept going. Jacques Piccard and Don Walsh. Their descent took four hours and forty-seven minutes. [S13]

## 10,935 Metres: The Bottom

- `s35` **[THE BOTTOM]** About three hours after you fell in, you touch down. Ten thousand nine hundred and thirty-five metres. The water is between one and four degrees. [S1, S4]
- `s36` **[Diatom ooze]** The floor is soft, pale ooze, made of the microscopic shells of tiny plankton that have been drifting down for thousands of years. You land in it like a cushion. [S13]
- `s37` **[Xenophyophores: single cells 10 cm+]** Your new neighbours include xenophyophores, single cells bigger than your hand. One cell. Ten centimetres. And they don't even have a brain. So, honestly, you have a lot in common right now. [S4]

## Who Eats You

- `s38` And then the guests arrive. Little shrimp-like scavengers called amphipods. Scientists' baited traps down here bring up huge numbers of them. [S1]
- `s39` **[Supergiant amphipods]** In two thousand fourteen, cameras in the Mariana Trench even filmed supergiant amphipods, much bigger than the usual ones. Down here, a body is a once in a lifetime buffet. [S4]
- `s40` **[100% had plastic inside]** And here's the bleakest fact in this video. When researchers checked amphipods from the deepest trenches, every single one had at least one piece of synthetic material in its stomach. [S4]
- `s41` You fell eleven kilometres to the most remote place on the planet. And our plastic still got here first.
- `s42` **[Whale fall]** When big bodies, like whales, reach the deep sea floor, they become feeding grounds for whole communities of creatures, for years. You're a smaller snack. But it's the same idea. Nothing down here goes to waste. [S18]

## Outro

- `s43` **[20 minutes on the bottom]** In nineteen sixty, Piccard and Walsh spent twenty minutes on the bottom. Then they went home. [S13]
- `s44` **[Permanent resident]** You are staying a bit longer.
- `s45` So, the deep ocean doesn't crush you into a cube. It drowns you near the top, chills you on the way, squeezes your air pockets, and then serves you to the locals with a side of microplastics. Five stars. Would not visit.
- `s46` **[NEXT: STRUCK BY LIGHTNING]** Next week, you get hit by three hundred million volts. Most people survive it. That's not the good news you think it is. See you there.

## Fact-check list

- The scenario (holding an anchor, sinking at ~1 m/s) is hypothetical and labelled on screen. Trieste averaged ≈0.63 m/s (10.9 km in 4 h 47 min); narration says we sink "a bit faster" — OK. Our 3-hour arrival = 10,935 m ÷ 1 m/s, an assumption.
- "Around sixty percent water": USGS (S19).
- Lung volume at 1,000 m ≈ 1/100 (Boyle's law at ~100 atm absolute ≈ 1/101) — derived from S3.
- Everest: 10,935 − 8,849 = 2,086 m → "more than two kilometres". OK.
- Supergiant amphipods "much bigger than the usual ones": S4 says "huge amphipods known as supergiants". OK.
- The 2023 record fish was in the Izu–Ogasawara Trench (near Japan), not the Mariana Trench. Narration says so.
- Ear pain within the first metres: well established in diving medicine; sourced via S7/S17.

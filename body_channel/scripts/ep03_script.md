# EP03 — What Happens to Your Body If You Get Struck by Lightning

_Your Vitals · narration script. Tags like [S3] link each claim to a source below._


## Hook

- `h01` **[300,000,000 VOLTS]** Three hundred million volts. Thirty thousand amps. Your wall socket gives you about one hundred and twenty volts. You just got the whole sky. [S1]
- `h02` **[5x hotter than the Sun]** The air around the bolt heats to around fifty thousand degrees Fahrenheit. That's about five times hotter than the surface of the Sun. [S2]
- `h03` **[9 in 10 SURVIVE]** And yet, about nine out of ten people struck by lightning survive. [S3]
- `h04` That sounds like the good news. It isn't, really. Because what lightning does to the survivors is the strangest part of this whole story. Let's go back thirty minutes, to the moment you made a terrible decision.

## T-Minus 30 Minutes

- `s01` It's a summer afternoon. You're on a golf course. There's a dark cloud way off on the horizon. It isn't even raining where you are.
- `s02` You hear a low rumble of thunder. You think, eh, it's miles away. Three more holes.
- `s03` **[Bolts from the blue: 10–15 miles]** Here's the problem. Lightning often strikes more than three miles from the centre of a storm, far outside the rain. So-called bolts from the blue can land ten to fifteen miles away. [S5]
- `s04` **[Hear thunder? You're in range.]** The National Weather Service puts it bluntly. If you can hear thunder, even a distant rumble, you are already in danger. [S5, S11]
- `s05` And standing in an open field, as the tallest thing around, holding a long stick, is exactly how you make yourself interesting.

## How It Finds You

- `s06` **[Side flash]** There are five main ways lightning gets you. A direct strike. A side flash, where it hits a tree and jumps to the person sheltering under it. Standing under a tree is the second leading cause of lightning casualties. [S4, S5]
- `s07` **[Ground current: most casualties]** Ground current, where the energy spreads out through the ground. That one causes the most lightning deaths and injuries, because it covers a much bigger area. [S4]
- `s08` Conduction, through wires and pipes. That's how people get hurt indoors, in the shower, or on a corded phone. And streamers, little sparks of electricity rising off the ground to meet the bolt. [S4]
- `s09` Today, you go for the classic. A direct strike. The rarest kind, but potentially the deadliest. [S4]

## 0.000 Seconds: The Strike

- `s10` **[FLASHOVER]** The bolt hits you, and something surprising happens. A lot of the current doesn't go through you. It races along the outside, just over your skin. This is called flashover. [S4]
- `s11` That's one reason lightning burns are usually not as bad as you'd expect. The real damage is done by the part of the current that goes inside, through your heart and your nervous system. [S4, S6]
- `s12` **[ASYSTOLE]** Your heart stops. Doctors call it asystole. The electrical rhythm that runs your heart just, resets. [S7]
- `s13` **[RESPIRATORY ARREST]** And the area of your brain that controls breathing gets knocked out too. You stop breathing. [S7]
- `s14` **[Heart restarts]** Now here's the twist. Your heart has its own built-in pacemaker, and the asystole may resolve by itself, fairly quickly. Your heart can start beating again on its own. [S7]
- `s15` **[Breathing stays off]** But breathing often stays switched off for longer. So your heart is back, beating away, pumping blood with no oxygen in it. That's what can kill you, a few minutes after you survived the actual lightning. [S7]
- `s16` **[SAFE TO TOUCH]** So if you ever see this happen, do something. Call emergency services, start CPR, use a defibrillator if there's one. And no, the victim doesn't stay electrified. The human body does not store electricity. It's safe to touch them. [S6, S8]
- `s17` Imagine dying because everyone was too scared to touch you. That's how much this myth matters. [S6]

## +1 Minute: The Aftermath

- `s18` **[Keraunoparalysis]** You wake up. But you can't move your legs. They're pale, maybe bluish, and nobody can find a pulse in them. [S7]
- `s19` **[Usually gone in hours]** It's called keraunoparalysis, a temporary paralysis caused by lightning. Terrifying. But it usually goes away within a few hours. [S7]
- `s20` Your ears are ringing. The blast can rupture your eardrums. [S7]
- `s21` **[LICHTENBERG FIGURES]** And on your skin, there's something beautiful. A pattern like a fern, or the branches of a tree, spreading across your body. They're called Lichtenberg figures. [S9]
- `s22` **[Fade in about a day]** They usually disappear within about twenty-four hours. Lightning gives you a tattoo, and then takes it back. [S9]
- `s23` So you're alive. You can walk. The burns are mild. The doctors run their scans, and the scans come back normal. You go home. Lucky you. Right?

## +1 Week: The Invisible Injury

- `s24` **[Mainly a nervous system injury]** Here's what most people get wrong. Lightning is mainly an injury to your nervous system. Your brain and your nerves. [S6]
- `s25` You get headaches. Nausea. You struggle to learn new information, and to pull up old information. You can't remember names, or what you were supposed to be doing at work. [S6]
- `s26` Your sleep goes strange too. Sometimes you sleep way too much at first, and later only two or three hours at a time. [S6]
- `s27` **[Scans: "normal"]** And the worst part? The x-rays and MRIs come back normal. Like a concussion, the problem is in how your brain works, not in how it looks on a picture. [S6]
- `s28` Your friends see the same person on the outside. They don't understand why you're different. Some stop calling. Some survivors pull away out of embarrassment. [S6]
- `s29` **[~75% with lasting problems]** Of the people who survive a lightning strike, about three in four have health problems that last. [S7]
- `s30` **[Cataracts, even a year later]** And some injuries show up late. Cataracts, a clouding of the lens in your eye, can appear more than a year after an otherwise uneventful recovery. [S7]

## The Man Struck Seven Times

- `s31` **[THE HUMAN LIGHTNING ROD]** Now, if you're thinking, once in a lifetime, fine. Let me introduce Roy Sullivan. [S10]
- `s32` **[Roy Sullivan, park ranger]** Roy was a park ranger at Shenandoah National Park in Virginia. Between nineteen forty-two and nineteen seventy-seven, he was struck by lightning seven times. Guinness World Records recognises him as the person struck the most recorded times. [S10]
- `s33` **[1942: lost his eyebrows]** Strike one, nineteen forty-two, at a new fire lookout tower that had no lightning rod. It burned off his eyebrows and eyelashes, and set his hair on fire. [S10]
- `s34` Later strikes hit him in his own front yard, and inside a ranger station. His hair caught fire again. And again. [S10]
- `s35` By the end, he believed storm clouds were following him. Once, he saw a cloud, tried to run away from it, and got struck anyway. He started carrying a can of water around, in case his hair caught fire. [S10]
- `s36` People started avoiding him, scared they'd get hit just by standing nearby. Which, if you think about it, is the loneliest possible side effect of being lucky.

## How To Not Be Roy

- `s37` So here's how to keep your vitals boring. There's no safe place outdoors in a thunderstorm. The only properly safe places are a substantial building or a hard topped metal vehicle. [S5, S11]
- `s38` **[Crouching doesn't help.]** Crouching down doesn't make you safe. Lying flat makes ground current worse. And don't shelter under a tree. Just get inside. [S5]
- `s39` **[Empire State Building: ~23 hits a year]** And lightning absolutely does strike the same place twice. The Empire State Building gets hit around twenty-three times a year. [S5]
- `s40` **[US: ~27 deaths/yr (2009–2018)]** The good news. In the United States, lightning deaths have dropped a lot, from about forty-three a year on average over three decades, to about twenty-seven a year in the decade to twenty eighteen. Your odds of being struck in a given year are around one in a million. [S3]
- `s41` **[Lifetime odds: ~1 in 15,300]** But over a whole lifetime, it's about one in fifteen thousand. And for every person struck, around ten people around them are affected too. [S3]

## Outro

- `s42` So, lightning doesn't usually kill you. It stops your heart, switches off your breathing, paints you like a fern, scrambles your memory, and then sends you home with a clean scan. Survival, it turns out, is complicated.
- `s43` When thunder roars, go indoors. Not after the next hole. Now.
- `s44` **[NEXT: 30 DAYS OF FAST FOOD]** Next week, you eat nothing but fast food for thirty days. We'll check your liver every week, and the scariest result shows up a year after you stop. See you then.

## Fact-check list

- The 50,000 °F figure is the temperature of the air in the lightning channel (NWS), not of the victim. Narration says "the air around the bolt" — OK.
- "Your wall socket gives you about 120 volts" is the US figure (NWS); much of the world uses 220–240 V. Consider adding "in the US" if the audience skews non-US.
- Roy Sullivan died in 1983 by suicide (Wikipedia). We deliberately don't mention or joke about it.
- Survival framing: "about nine out of ten survive" (NWS 10% fatality).
- "Respiratory arrest is what can kill a few minutes later" is our explanation of S7's asystole vs prolonged respiratory arrest; consistent with S3 (cardiac arrest, delayed deaths from brain damage).

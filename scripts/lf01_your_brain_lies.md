# Long-form 01: "Your Brain Lies to You Every Day"

- **Target length:** about 10 minutes, roughly 1,450 words at 145 words per minute.
- **Format:** 16:9 long-form, with handwritten margin-note style captions.
- **Thumbnail:** `style/reference_frames/thumb.png`. Big handwritten "Your brain **lies** to you every day" next to the scribble-head figure.
- **Title options (A/B test with YouTube's Test & Compare):**
  1. Your Brain Lies to You Every Day (7 Proofs)
  2. 7 Ways Your Brain Is Lying to You Right Now
  3. Your Brain Is Not on Your Side

Structure: a cold open, 7 glitches, then an ending that loops back to the cold open. Each glitch runs about 70–80 seconds and follows the same beats: **the moment you recognise** → **the study** → **why your brain does it** → **a one-line takeaway**. Glitches 3 and 7 are the most surprising, so they sit at the points where viewers usually drop off (around 25% and 85% of the way through).

Legend:
- `[V]` visual
- `[T]` on-screen handwritten text. **Bold** marks the yellow keyword.
- `→` margin-note source

## 0:00 Cold open (about 35s)
[V] Black screen. A stick figure walks into a room through a doorway and freezes. A yellow "?" draws itself above its head.

> You walk into the kitchen. You stop. And you have no idea why you're there.
> You didn't forget. Your brain deleted it, on purpose.
> And that's not even the strangest thing it does to you. Today: seven ways your brain lies to you every single day, and the experiments that caught it doing it.

[T] Your brain **lies** to you.
[V] The scribble cloud from the logo explodes above the figure's head, then the title card appears: "overthought".

## 0:35 Glitch 1: The doorway effect (about 70s)
[V] Scene `s1_doorway` and `s2_brainfiles`.
> Back to that kitchen. In 2011, psychologist Gabriel Radvansky at the University of Notre Dame had people carry objects through rooms, first in a virtual world and later in real ones. Some walked through a doorway; others walked the same distance in a single room.
> The people who went through a door forgot more. Same distance, same time. The only difference was the door.
> Here's why. Your brain doesn't record life as one long video. It cuts it into chapters, called event boundaries, and a doorway is a perfect "new chapter" signal. So it files the last room away, along with the reason you came.

[T] Doorways **reset** your memory. → Radvansky · Notre Dame · 2011
> The fix sounds silly, but it works: walk back through the door. Going back into the old setting brings the memory back.

[V] `s3_lightbulb`. The figure walks back and a yellow lightbulb switches on.

## 1:45 Glitch 2: The spotlight effect (about 75s)
[V] `s4d_eyeballs`. The figure trips and eyeballs pop in one by one around it.
> You trip on the stairs. Your face goes hot. Everyone saw.
> Except they didn't. In 2000, Thomas Gilovich at Cornell asked students to walk into a room full of people while wearing a T-shirt with a big picture of Barry Manilow on it. To a college student in 2000, that was deeply embarrassing.
> The students guessed that about half the room noticed the shirt. The real number was about a quarter.

[T] Only **1 in 4** noticed. → Gilovich · Cornell · 2000
> You are the main character in your own head, so your brain assumes you're the main character in everyone else's. But everyone else is busy starring in their own movie, and worrying about their own shirt.

[V] `s5c_eyespop`. The eyeballs pop into yellow sparks and the figure relaxes.

## 3:00 Glitch 3: You miss things right in front of you (about 85s)
[V] Six stick figures pass a ball around, three in white and three in grey.
> Quick test. Count how many times the white team passes the ball.
[V] About 6 seconds of passing. A large stick-figure gorilla walks slowly through the middle and thumps its chest.
> Got a number? Good. Did you see the gorilla?
> In 1999, Daniel Simons and Christopher Chabris ran this with real video, real players and a person in a gorilla suit walking straight through the game. About half the people counting passes never saw the gorilla. Many refused to believe it until they watched the video again.

[T] Half missed the **gorilla**. → Simons & Chabris · Harvard · 1999
> It's called inattentional blindness. Your brain can't process everything, so it spotlights what you're focused on and quietly drops the rest. You don't experience the world. You experience your brain's edited summary of it.
> And that matters well beyond gorillas: phones and driving, missing a friend waving at you, or not noticing a typo you've read five times.

## 4:25 Glitch 4: The same car everywhere (about 65s)
[V] The figure in a car. One yellow car passes, then suddenly yellow cars are everywhere.
> You buy a new car, and suddenly that car is everywhere. Or you learn a new word, and you hear it three times that week.
> Those cars were always there. What changed is your brain. It just tagged that car as important, so it starts flagging it. Then a second trick kicks in: each sighting feels like proof that it's happening more.
> Linguist Arnold Zwicky named this the frequency illusion in 2005. Most people know it as the Baader–Meinhof phenomenon.

[T] It was **always** there. → Zwicky · Stanford · 2005
> Remember this one when you start seeing "signs" everywhere. Sometimes it's a sign. Usually it's your filter.

## 5:30 Glitch 5: Faces in everything (about 60s)
[V] A power socket, a car front and a slice of toast each grow simple faces.
> Look at this power socket. It's shocked. This car looks angry. This toast looks… holy.
> Seeing faces in objects is called pareidolia. In 2014, researchers in Toronto and China showed people pure visual noise and told them a face might be hidden in it. A lot of people saw one. Brain scans showed their face-recognition area lit up as if a real face were there.

[T] Your brain **wants** faces. → Liu et al. · Cortex · 2014
> For our ancestors, missing a face in the bushes could be fatal, while seeing one that wasn't there cost nothing. So evolution built a brain that would rather be wrong a hundred times than miss one real face.
> That study won an Ig Nobel Prize, the award for research that first makes you laugh and then makes you think.

## 6:30 Glitch 6: The ending rewrites the whole story (about 75s)
[V] A timeline line. The figure walks along it with a mood graph above, dipping and rising.
> Think of your best holiday. Now think of one that ended with a cancelled flight. Notice how the bad ending poisons the whole memory?
> In 1993, Daniel Kahneman, who later won a Nobel Prize, had people hold a hand in painfully cold water. Once for 60 seconds. Once for 60 seconds, then 30 more while the water warmed up very slightly.
> The second trial was strictly worse: the same pain, plus more pain. But when asked which one to repeat, most people chose the longer one.

[T] Your memory keeps the **peak** and the **end**. → Kahneman et al. · 1993
> Your brain doesn't store the average of an experience. It stores the most intense moment and the last moment, then throws away the rest. It's called the peak-end rule.
> Use it: end a date, a meeting or a workout on a high note, and that's what everyone remembers. Including you.

## 7:45 Glitch 7: Your memories aren't recordings (about 90s)
[V] A memory drawn as a film strip. Frames start redrawing themselves.
> Here's the big one. You trust your memory because it feels like a recording.
> In 1974, Elizabeth Loftus and John Palmer showed people a film of a car crash, then asked one question with one word changed. "How fast were the cars going when they **hit** each other?" or "…when they **smashed** into each other?"
> The people who heard "smashed" estimated faster speeds. Then, a week later, researchers asked everyone: "Did you see any broken glass?"

[V] Pause beat. A yellow scribble cloud forms.
> There was no broken glass in the film. But about a third of the "smashed" group remembered seeing it, roughly twice as many as the "hit" group.

[T] One word **created** a memory. → Loftus & Palmer · 1974
> Every time you remember something, you rebuild it, and you can rebuild it slightly wrong. A question, a photo, a story someone told you: any of it can edit the file. Loftus spent decades showing that whole false memories can be planted this way. Her work changed how courts treat eyewitness testimony.
> So when you're sure that's how it happened, your brain might just be very confident.

## 9:15 Ending and loop (about 45s)
[V] All seven scenes flash by as small thumbnails, then return to the figure in the doorway.
> So your brain deletes, edits, filters and rewrites. Is it broken?
> No. It's doing exactly what it evolved to do: take shortcuts, so you can get through a world with too much information. Most of the time, the shortcuts work. The trick is knowing when they don't.
> And now you know seven.

[T] Your brain isn't broken. It's **taking shortcuts.**
> If this made you rethink something, subscribe. Every week, one more glitch, with the receipts.
> Oh, and the reason you walked into that kitchen…

[V] The figure in the doorway again, with the yellow "?". Cut to black, which loops back to the cold open.

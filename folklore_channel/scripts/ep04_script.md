# EP04 — How to Survive the Wild Hunt (According to Folklore)

Narration as recorded (generated from the film file). Ward cards in **bold**.


## Hook

It's the week after Christmas, a long time ago, and you're walking home late. The village is still a mile away. And the wind has just changed.

First, the dogs in the farmyards go quiet. Then, high above you, you hear them. Hounds. Dozens of hounds, baying in the sky. Horns. Hooves on nothing. And something like laughter in the wind.

Look up, and you might see them. Riders on black horses, crossing the clouds. Some of them are dead. Some of them are people you used to know. And the one at the front, the hunter, is looking for company.

All over Europe, from Norway to the Alps to the English fens, people told stories about this. They called it the furious host. The ghostly ride. The Wild Hunt.

> *They kill anyone they meet who does not throw himself down with his face to the ground. — a Bohemian legend, 1863*

And almost everywhere, they told you the same thing. If it catches you on the road, you have seconds to decide what to do.

This is the Ward Book. The last chapter of the year. And tonight, how to survive the Wild Hunt.


## Who Rides

Who's leading it depends on where you live. In Germany and Scandinavia, it was often Wodan, or Odin. The one-eyed god, galloping through the storm with the souls of the dead behind him.

That link was made famous by Jacob Grimm. Yes, the fairy tale Grimm. In eighteen thirty-five, in his huge book on German mythology, he gathered the legends of the furious host and argued they were a memory of the old god's ride.

But the riders change from country to country. In England, a twelfth-century writer named Walter Map told of King Herla, who spent three days at a fairy wedding, came home to find two hundred years had passed, and has been riding ever since.

In Windsor, it was Herne the Hunter, the antlered ghost that Shakespeare put into The Merry Wives of Windsor, walking round an oak at midnight in the dead of winter.

And in a Dutch legend, a lord who loved hunting more than anything is cursed by his own dying father. Hunt then forever, the old man cries. Aye, forever. And so he does.


## Ward I

**WARD I: Know the Twelve Nights**

Ward one. Know the Twelve Nights.

The Hunt can ride on any wild night, especially in autumn and winter. But in the German-speaking Alps, one stretch of the year is its favourite. The Rauhnächte. The twelve nights between Christmas and Epiphany.

It's the time between the years. The old year is dead, and the new one hasn't properly started. In that gap, the stories say, the door between worlds hangs open. So be home before dark, close the shutters, and keep the candles lit.


## Ward Ii

**WARD II: Never call out to the Hunt**

Ward two. Never, ever call out to the Hunt.

This is the most common warning in the whole legend. And the stories are honestly a little bit funny, in a horrible way. In a Dutch tale, a drunk man hears the hunt pass over, and he shouts up, Hey, Sir Hunter! Give me part of your game!

The next morning, his wife opens the front door, and finds the hind quarter of an ox hanging there. He asked for a share. He got one.

And it gets worse. In a Bohemian telling, anyone who calls back finds the back half of a horse thrown through their window. On the island of Rügen, in Germany, a worker who hollered along with the hunt had a woman's leg thrown at him. A leg still wearing a red shoe.

The lesson is very clear. The Hunt is not a parade. Don't cheer. Don't heckle. Don't ask for souvenirs. Whatever it's hunting, you do not want a slice of it.


## Ward Iii

**WARD III: Face down, and don't look up**

Ward three. Throw yourself face down, and don't look up.

This one comes straight from the legends of Bohemia, recorded in eighteen sixty-three. A peasant on a forest road hears the hunt coming. He throws himself down with his face to the ground, and the whole procession passes him by. Not a scratch.

A tradesman on the same road does the same thing. But he gets curious. He lifts his head, just for a moment, to watch them go by. And he goes mad.

Another telling from the north of Germany adds one more detail. The huntsman spares those who stay in the very middle of the path. So, middle of the road, face in the dirt, eyes shut. It's undignified. It's also how you get home.


## Ward Iv

**WARD IV: Take nothing from the Hunt**

Ward four. Take nothing from the Hunt.

On the night of the first of January, ten ninety-one, a young priest named Walchelin was walking home in Normandy, when a huge army of the dead passed him on the road. Peasants, women, monks, knights. Some of them he recognised. Some had died not long before.

Walchelin had a very modern thought. Nobody is going to believe me. I need proof. So he grabbed the reins of a horse with no rider.

The stirrup burned his foot. The reins froze his hand. And a dead knight grabbed him by the throat, and left a mark that the monk Orderic Vitalis said he carried for the rest of his life. Orderic wrote the story down, and claimed he'd heard it from Walchelin himself.

So, if the Hunt drops something on the road, a horse, a horn, a coin, leave it. It's not lost. It's bait.


## Ward V

**WARD V: Take the washing in**

And the last ward. Take the washing in.

This one is still practised, half as a joke and half not, in parts of Germany and Austria today. During the Twelve Nights, don't hang your laundry outside.

Why? The explanations vary. Some say the riders get tangled in it, and you don't want them stuck in your garden. Others say a sheet the Hunt rides through becomes a shroud. And whoever sleeps in it will be dead before the year is out.

So, if your grandmother in Bavaria refuses to do the washing between Christmas and New Year, now you know. She isn't being lazy. She's being careful.


## The Hunt In The Records

And here's the part that gives me chills. The Wild Hunt shows up in a real English historical record. The Peterborough Chronicle, a version of the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, kept by monks.

In the entry for eleven twenty-seven, the monk writes that many men saw and heard huntsmen hunting. They were black, and big, and loathsome. Their hounds were black, with wide staring eyes. And they rode on black horses, and black goats.

They were seen, he says, in the deer park of Peterborough and in the woods all the way to Stamford, and the monks heard horns blowing at night. It began right when a new abbot arrived. A man named Henry of Poitou, whom the monks absolutely hated.

Was it a real sighting? A storm? Or a furious monk, using the oldest ghost story in Europe to say exactly what he thought of his new boss? Historians lean towards the last one. But it's still the earliest Wild Hunt in English writing.


## What Was Really Up There

So what were people actually hearing? Part of the answer may be flying overhead. In the north of England, the baying in the sky had its own name, Gabriel's hounds. And many people explained it as wild geese, migrating at night, honking in the dark like a pack of dogs.

Add winter storms howling through the forest, a world with no street lights, and a deep Christian fear of restless souls of the dead. And you have the perfect conditions for an army in the clouds.

And here's the twist I promised. In medieval France, one leader of the hunt was a demon called Hellequin. Over the centuries, his name drifted onto the stage, into Italian comedy, and he slowly became Harlequin. The clown in the diamond suit.

So the king of the dead, who galloped across the sky collecting souls, ended up as a jester. Which is, honestly, the most folklore thing that has ever happened.


## The Verdict

So, how do you survive the Wild Hunt? Know the Twelve Nights. Never call out. Face down in the middle of the road, and don't look up. Take nothing it leaves behind. And bring in the washing.

Those wards kept people indoors, together, quiet and careful, on the darkest, coldest nights of the year. Which, if you think about it, was very good advice, ghosts or no ghosts.

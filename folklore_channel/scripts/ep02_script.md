# EP02 — How to Survive the Wendigo (According to Folklore)

Narration as recorded (generated from the film file). Ward cards in **bold**.


## Hook

It's February, somewhere in the northern forest. The snow is deeper than you are tall. And the food ran out eleven days ago.

You've boiled your moccasin leather. You've scraped lichen off the rocks. And tonight, for the first time, sitting by the fire, you look at the person next to you, and you notice a thought that frightens you.

The people who have lived in these forests for thousands of years have a name for what that thought can turn into.

Windigo. Wendigo. Witiko. A giant of hunger, made of frost and bone, that eats people, and is never, ever full.

And here's what makes it different from almost every other monster in the Ward Book. You don't only meet the Wendigo out in the woods. Sometimes, you become it.

This is the Ward Book. And tonight, how to survive the Wendigo.


## Whose Legend This Is

First, whose story is this? The Wendigo belongs to Algonquian-speaking peoples of the northern forests. The Anishinaabe, or Ojibwe. The Cree. The Innu. And their neighbours around the Great Lakes and across the subarctic.

For them, this isn't a movie monster. It's a teaching, passed down for generations, and it's still told today. So tonight we're leaning on Indigenous writers and careful historians, and we're going to treat it that way.

The Anishinaabe writer Basil Johnston described the Weendigo as a giant. Gaunt to the point of starvation. Skin stretched over its bones, the grey colour of ash. Lips torn and bloody.

And here's the detail that matters most. Every time the Weendigo eats, it grows. So its hunger grows with it. It can never catch up. It's starving forever, no matter how much it eats.

Johnston saw the lesson clearly. The Weendigo is what excess looks like. Greed, with a body. A hunger that destroys everything it touches, including itself.


## Where The Fear Came From

To understand the Wendigo, you have to understand winter in the subarctic, before roads, before planes, before grocery stores.

In a bad winter, the animals vanish. Snow traps families in camps far from anyone else. Historians who study the fur trade era have found record after record of hunger so severe that people starved to death in their own lodges.

And in that kind of hunger, there is one taboo that sits above every other. You do not eat another human being. Not ever. The Wendigo is that taboo, given a face.


## Ward I

**WARD I: Share your food**

Ward one. Share your food.

This is the deepest rule of all, and it's not about the monster. It's about you. The Wendigo is a spirit of greed. Of keeping more than you need while others go without.

In a world where survival depended on everyone, hoarding food was the first step toward becoming something inhuman. So the first ward is the simplest one. When you have food, share it.


## Ward Ii

**WARD II: Never cross the line**

Ward two. Never cross the line. No matter what.

In many tellings, the surest way to become a Wendigo is to eat human flesh. Even once. Even starving. Even if the person was already dead.

It's a brutal rule, born in brutal winters. But it gave people something to hold on to when everything else had gone. Death, the stories said, is better than becoming the Wendigo.


## Ward Iii

**WARD III: Watch yourself, not just the woods**

Ward three. Watch yourself, not just the woods.

The scariest thing about the Wendigo is that the first signs are inside your own head. In some accounts, the person turning feels sick at the sight of normal food. They grow cold. Distant. And they start to look at people the way a hunter looks at game.

So communities watched for these signs. And historians have recorded something heartbreaking. People who believed they were turning would sometimes ask their own families to kill them, before they could hurt anyone.

Think about that. The ward isn't a charm you hang on your door. It's paying attention to yourself, and caring enough about the people around you to stop.


## Ward Iv

**WARD IV: Melt the heart of ice**

Ward four. Melt the heart of ice.

In many accounts, a Wendigo has a heart of ice. That's the frozen centre of the monster, and the key to beating it.

The stories say it can only truly be destroyed by fire, or by melting that frozen heart. Some tellings speak of hot fat, poured down the throat of someone who was turning, to melt the ice inside before it could take over.

Whatever you make of the cure, the symbol is clear. Hunger and greed freeze the heart. Warmth, literal and human, is what thaws it.


## Ward V

**WARD V: Trust the ones who fight it**

Ward five. Trust the ones who know how to fight it.

In the early nineteen hundreds, one of the most famous was Jack Fiddler, an Oji-Cree leader at Sandy Lake, in what's now northern Ontario. He was said to have defeated many Wendigos in his lifetime.

And that's where this legend runs straight into Canadian law. In June nineteen oh seven, the North-West Mounted Police arrested Jack Fiddler and his brother Joseph, for killing a woman their community believed was turning into a Wendigo.

Jack Fiddler took his own life in custody that September. Joseph was convicted, and died in custody in nineteen oh nine. To the police, it was murder. To their community, it was protection. Two worlds, two laws, and one terrible winter story caught between them.


## The Real Cases

There's another real case people always bring up. In eighteen seventy-nine, a Cree man known as Swift Runner was hanged at Fort Saskatchewan, in today's Alberta.

He'd killed and eaten members of his own family that winter, and he said a Wendigo spirit had taken hold of him. But the police noted something important. There was food nearby. This wasn't simple starvation.

Cases like this led twentieth-century psychiatrists to invent a diagnosis. Wendigo psychosis. A supposed madness, unique to northern peoples, where a person craves human flesh.

But in nineteen eighty-two, the anthropologist Lou Marano looked at the records closely, and argued that most so-called Wendigo cases were something else entirely. Not cannibals. People accused of turning, who were killed by frightened communities before they could.

So the real horror of the Wendigo might not be a monster in the trees. It might be what fear and hunger do to a group of people, trapped together, in the longest night of the year.


## What About The Antlers?

Quick myth check. If you've seen a Wendigo in a game or a horror film, it probably had a deer skull and huge antlers.

Look in the old accounts, and the antlers aren't there. Johnston's Weendigo is a starving giant with torn lips, not a deer. The antlers are a modern addition, from horror movies and video games. Spooky, sure. But it isn't the legend.


## The Verdict

So how do you survive the Wendigo? Strangely, this is the one legend where none of the wards are about running, or hiding, or salt, or iron.

Share your food. Never cross the line. Watch yourself. Keep your heart warm. And listen to the people who know these woods.

The Wendigo is a warning about what happens when anyone, a person, a family, a whole society, takes more than it needs and still wants more. And that, honestly, is a monster that never went away.

Next week, we leave the frozen forest for a darker kind of wood. A hut that stands on chicken legs. A fence made of bones. And an old woman who might help you, or might put you in the oven.

It's Baba Yaga. And she has very particular rules about the questions you're allowed to ask. Subscribe, so the Ward Book is open when she comes. And until then, share your dinner.

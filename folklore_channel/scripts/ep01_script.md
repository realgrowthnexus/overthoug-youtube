# EP01 — How to Survive Krampus (According to Folklore)

Narration as recorded (generated from the film file). Ward cards in **bold**.


## Hook

It is the fifth of December, somewhere high in the Alps. The sun went down at half past four, and every door in the village is already bolted.

You're seven years old. And out in the street, you can hear it. Bells. Heavy iron bells. And under the bells, the drag of a chain on frozen stone.

Something with goat horns, black fur and a tongue as long as your arm is walking from house to house. It carries a bundle of birch sticks. And on its back there's a basket, big enough for a child.

Tonight, it's looking for you. Not for being evil. For being naughty. And the old stories are very clear about what happens to naughty children who end up in that basket.

> *Some say he drowns them. Some say he eats them. Some say he carries them straight to Hell. — the Alpine tellings*

Some say he drowns them. Some say he eats them. Some say he carries them straight down to Hell.

But here's the thing. Alpine families have survived this night for hundreds of years. They have rules. Five of them. Follow them, and you'll see the sixth of December. Break one, and, well. Let's find out.

This is the Ward Book. And tonight, how to survive Krampus.


## Who Is Krampus

First, know your enemy. Krampus belongs to the Eastern Alps. Austria, Bavaria, South Tyrol, and the mountain valleys around them, where winter nights are long and very, very dark.

His name probably comes from the old German word Krampen, which means claw. Which tells you a lot about how this relationship is going to go.

He's usually drawn half goat, half demon. Horns. Hooves. Shaggy fur. A long, pointed, ridiculous tongue. Chains around his body, and bells that you hear long before you see him.

And he carries a rute. A bundle of birch branches, for swatting children. Some families say it's a warning. Some say it's a punishment. The children say it's both.

But the rod is not the worst part. The worst part is the basket on his back. That's the exit for kids who are beyond saving.

Now here's the strangest part of the whole legend. Krampus never travels alone. He works for a saint.

On the sixth of December, the Church celebrates Saint Nicholas, the kind bishop who brings presents. And on the eve of his feast, the fifth, Nicholas goes from house to house. Good children get apples, nuts and sweets.

And the naughty ones? That's what the assistant is for. The saint does the rewards. The demon does the paperwork.


## Ward I

**WARD I: Know which night it is**

Ward one. Know which night it is.

Krampusnacht, Krampus Night, is the fifth of December. That's his night. Not Christmas Eve, not Halloween. The fifth. Everywhere else in the world, this is a normal Saturday. In the Alps, it's the night the doors get bolted.

So the first rule is simply this. If you're anywhere in the Eastern Alps in early December, check the calendar. If it says five, be indoors early, be nice to your siblings, and for heaven's sake, eat your vegetables.


## Ward Ii

**WARD II: Never meet him without the saint**

Ward two. Never meet Krampus without the saint.

In the old house visits, the two arrive together. Nicholas walks in first, calm and holy. Krampus comes in behind him, rattling, growling, and very much on a leash.

And look at those chains. Many folklorists read them as a symbol of the Christian Church binding the Devil. Krampus is dangerous, yes. But he's a chained danger. He goes where the saint lets him go.

So as long as Saint Nicholas is in the room, you are, technically, under protection. The danger is the stories where Krampus turns up first. Alone. Without his boss.


## Ward Iii

**WARD III: Have your answer ready**

Ward three. Have your answer ready.

When the saint arrives, he asks questions. Have you been good this year? Do you know your prayers? Have you listened to your parents? In some families, the bishop even reads from a big golden book of everything you did.

Children were expected to say a prayer, or a poem, or at least promise to behave. A good answer means presents. A bad answer means the saint steps aside, and lets the assistant have a word.

So rehearse. Out loud. In front of a mirror. You are about to have the most important job interview of your life, and the interviewer has horns.


## Ward Iv

**WARD IV: Keep the golden rod on the wall**

Ward four. Keep the golden rod on the wall.

Here's a detail most people never hear. In some Alpine homes, the birch rute isn't thrown away after Krampus Night. It gets painted gold and hung up in the house, where it stays all year.

Why? It's a reminder. Every time you misbehave in, say, July, somebody points at the wall. No words needed. The demon may be gone, but his invoice is still hanging in the kitchen.

Think of it as a ward that works in reverse. It doesn't keep Krampus out. It keeps you from ever needing that basket.


## Ward V

**WARD V: On the night of the run, stay off the street**

Ward five. On the night of the run, stay off the street.

Today, in many Alpine towns, Krampus doesn't just visit houses. He runs. The Krampuslauf, the Krampus run, is a parade of young men in fur suits and hand-carved wooden masks, charging through the streets with bells and torches.

Some of those masks take weeks to carve and weigh several kilos. The suits are made of real goat or sheep fur. And quite a few of the runners have had a few glasses of schnapps to keep warm.

And the rods are real. Runners swat at the crowd, usually on the legs. Locals know to stand back, or to stand behind someone taller. Tourists learn the hard way.

So the final ward is the most modern one. Krampus Night is a party now. But it's a party where the guests carry sticks.


## Where He Really Comes From

So where did Krampus really come from? This is where it gets interesting, because nobody is completely sure.

In nineteen fifty-eight, the folklorist Maurice Bruce wrote that Krampus in Styria, a region of Austria, looked much older than Christianity. A horned winter spirit, from the days when mid-winter belonged to the dark.

Others are more careful. The clearer evidence comes from Christian Nicholas plays and house visits, where a devil character follows the saint around. Krampus may be an ancient spirit that got baptised. Or a stage devil who got very, very popular.

Either way, the partnership stuck. A kind saint and a terrifying servant. Reward and punishment, walking side by side, down every snowy street.


## The One Thing Krampus Feared

And here's the twist. For centuries, nothing could stop Krampus. Not priests, not parents, not the Enlightenment. And then came the nineteen thirties.

After nineteen thirty-two, Austria's authoritarian government, under Engelbert Dollfuss, turned against the Krampus tradition. In nineteen thirty-four, the New York Times even reported that Krampus was disliked in Fascist Austria.

Did it work? Not really. These are remote mountain valleys. You cannot police every village on the fifth of December. Krampus simply went back to doing what he does best. Walking from house to house in the dark.

Long before that, he'd already conquered the post office. From the eighteen hundreds, Austrians sent Krampus cards. Greetings from Krampus. Horned demons, chasing children, and the message, basically, happy holidays.

And today he's more popular than ever. Krampus runs have spread across Europe and to America, with Hollywood films, beer labels, and costume clubs that spend all year building the scariest suit in the valley.


## The Verdict

So, how do you survive Krampus? Honestly, the folklore's answer is a little annoying. You don't fight him. You don't trap him. You behave.

Krampus was never really a monster you defeat. He's a reminder. A way for long, dark winters to teach children that what you do all year comes due at the end of it.

So, know the night. Stay near the saint. Have your answer ready. Respect the golden rod. And when you hear bells on a December street, take one step back from the kerb.

That's five wards, from one of the oldest winter legends in Europe. Next week, we go somewhere much colder, to a monster that isn't sent by a saint, and doesn't care if you've been good.

It's the creature of hunger. The Wendigo. And its rules are very different. Subscribe, so the Ward Book is open when it comes. And until then, stay inside on the fifth.
